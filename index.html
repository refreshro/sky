☀️ Sun and Moon · Orbital Clock

A visual exercise in kinematics: the Sun turns 360° a day, the Moon follows its own cycle.

Notes

The Sun (360°/day) The Moon (synodic cycle ~29.53 days)

The 24-hour dial: the first circle in the readings panel is the observer's sky at the chosen place over one whole civil day, from midnight (bottom) to noon (top). The thick ring is the Sun: gold while it is above the horizon, orange in civil twilight (down to 6° below the horizon), blue in nautical (12°) and astronomical (18°) twilight, dark at night. The thin ring shows when the Moon is above the horizon. The part of the day already gone is dimmed. The hand is civil time, the gold triangle is true solar noon, and the white ticks are sunrise and sunset. True noon rarely falls at 12:00, because of longitude, the time zone and the equation of time.

Sunrise and sunset: computed for a geometric altitude of zero, without atmospheric refraction and without the disc's semi-diameter. That is why they can come a few minutes later at sunrise and earlier at sunset than in official tables. Moonrise and moonset also ignore the Moon's parallax.

Solar time: computed from the Sun's position on the disc (15° = 1h) and includes the equation of time. The day / twilight / night state comes from the Sun's real altitude at the chosen place. For a custom place without a latitude, the state is approximated from longitude.

The control panel: three rows, one per role. Time: LIVE (real time, the default at start), play backward, pause, play forward, plus the speed slider, logarithmic, from one minute to one year per second (m/s, h/s, d/s: model minutes, hours, days per real second; the mouse wheel on the dial fine-tunes it) and three presets: 10 m/s, 1 h/s, 1 d/s. The moment: date and time in the chosen place's time zone; Set (or Enter) takes the model there instantly and leaves it paused, so you see exactly the moment you asked for. Next to them, the place. The view: Diurnal / Synodic and the drawn layers. On the keyboard: Space pause / play, L live, the arrows one hour per step (with Shift one day, with Alt one minute), always paused.

The starting point: the default place is Greenwich, on the prime meridian, at GMT (UTC+0, no daylight saving time), the origin of both longitude and the model's clock. London, in the list, follows British civil time, with summer time.

Moon phase: the percentage is the illuminated fraction of the disc, (1 − cos φ) / 2, where φ is the position in the synodic cycle. That is why the Moon goes quickly from 50% to almost 100%, while around full moon the percentage barely changes.

The Moon's horns: the preview in the readings panel shows the phase as an observer at the chosen place sees it, with the top of the page towards the zenith. The angle is computed from the altitude and azimuth of the Sun and the Moon, so it turns during the evening exactly as it turns in the sky. The marker on the dial stays turned towards the Sun, because there the drawing is a map seen from above, not a view from the eye.

Declination: positive north of the celestial equator, negative south. The Sun stays within ±23.44°, because it moves along the ecliptic. The Moon reaches ±28.6°, because its orbit is tilted 5.14° to the ecliptic. That is why the Moon's marker can go inside the Tropic of Cancer and the Sun's cannot.

The projection: azimuthal equidistant, with the North Pole at the centre. A point's radius is its true distance to the pole, i.e. (90 − latitude) degrees of arc. Distances from the centre are therefore exact, while those between any two cities are not. One degree of arc = 111.32 km.

Reference circles: the celestial equator, the tropics (Cancer to the north, Capricorn to the south, ±23.44°), the polar circles (Arctic and Antarctic, ±66.56°). They are drawn on the disc without labels, so they do not overlap.

Compass and Sky: on a phone, Compass rotates the dial with the device's orientation, so the direction towards the pole from your place points to true north; held flat, everything on the map lies in the direction in which you see it on the horizon. The phone's compass shows magnetic north, and the correction (default +1°, for Greenwich) brings it to true north. My location sets the place from the device's position. Without a sensor (computer), the dial turns towards the south, with south at the top. Held overhead, screen down, the phone shows the sky correctly with no mirroring at all: east stays on the left when your back is to the north, because turning the phone over does the mirroring by itself. Sky is for a screen looked at normally, on a computer or with the phone in hand: the map is mirrored and rotated as for someone with their back to the north looking up, so north is at the top, south at the bottom, east on the left, and the constellation figures appear as in the sky. The gold arrowhead on the edge of the dial always shows the chosen place's north: on the line from the place through the North Pole, on the side opposite the place. With Compass on, Sky is inactive. With both off, the dial returns to its usual form.

Stars and constellations: the white dots are the brightest stars (down to magnitude 3.5), larger the brighter they are. The blue lines and names are the real zodiac constellations, the 12 plus Ophiuchus. Positions are corrected for precession (rigorous IAU rotation) to the displayed date. The map is seen from above, like a globe: the direction and altitude of every star from the chosen place are correct, but the figures appear mirrored compared with how you see them looking up. Compared with the gold sign glyphs, you can see the offset of almost one sign brought by precession. The layer is turned on from the Stars pill (off at start; the data loads only then). Data: Yale Bright Star Catalogue, via d3-celestial (Olaf Frohn, BSD licence).

Signs on the dial: the gold glyphs sit on the Sun's path (the ecliptic), in the middle of each sign, and turn with the sky. The bright ones are above the chosen place's horizon, the faint ones below it; in the evening you see the ones rising in the east. They are turned on and off from the Signs pill (off at start).

Sign and constellation: in the Sun and Moon cards, "On the ecliptic" shows the zodiac sign, an equal 30° sector counted from the March equinox, and the constellation in front of which the body stands in the sky, by the official boundaries. The two differ by almost a whole sign: the equinox has shifted ~24° since the signs were fixed (precession). The constellations have unequal widths and there are 13 on the ecliptic, with Ophiuchus between Scorpius and Sagittarius: the Sun crosses Scorpius in about 7 days, Virgo in about 45. The Moon, which strays up to ~5° from the ecliptic, can also pass through corners of Cetus, Orion, Sextans, Corvus or Auriga.

Seasons and hemispheres: the season and the names of the equinoxes and solstices follow the chosen place's hemisphere. The moments are the same for the whole Earth, but in the southern hemisphere the labels swap: the December solstice is the summer solstice there, and the March equinox is the autumn one.

Analemma: the vertical is the Sun's declination, from −23.44° to +23.44°; the horizontal is the equation of time, from −14.2 to +16.4 minutes. The white dot is the Sun at the current date, and the ticks mark the 1st of each month, as on the noon-mark sundials on walls. The top line is the Tropic of Cancer, the bottom one the Tropic of Capricorn, and the middle one the celestial equator. The two axes have equal scales, so the figure 8 has its true proportions: 46.88° tall and only 7.66° wide. The two causes combined in it: the obliquity of the ecliptic gives the vertical swing, and the Earth's varying orbital speed gives the horizontal one.

Eclipses: the vertical is the Moon's ecliptic latitude at new moon. Zero means the Moon passes exactly in front of the Sun. The band marks the 1.5° threshold: below it an eclipse happens somewhere on Earth, and the closer the dot is to zero, the deeper the eclipse. The horizontal covers 13 consecutive new moons, from six months back to six ahead, with the dotted line at the current date. Eclipses do not come evenly: they cluster in seasons, about 173 days apart, and the dots that drop into the band are eclipses.

Cycles: each circle is a cycle, with a marker at the current phase. The markers never line up, because the periods are incommensurable. The day: 24h. The synodic month: 29.53 days, from new moon to new moon. The tropical year: 365.24 days, from equinox to equinox. The eclipse year: 346.62 days, the time the Sun takes to return to the same node. The nodal cycle: 18.6 years, the time the node takes to go round the ecliptic.

Metonic: 235 synodic months make 6939.688 days, and 19 tropical years make 6939.602. The difference: 0.086 days, about two hours in 19 years. You can check it by hand: note the Moon's phase at a date, then set the date to exactly 19 years later and compare. The phase comes back close to where it started, off by those two hours.

Rotations and lunations: the model counts how many apparent rotations the Sun and the Moon have made since the epoch, and how far the Moon has fallen behind. The lunar day is 24h50m, longer than the solar one, so the lag grows by exactly one cycle every synodic month. Both quantities were taken out of the readings panel: their absolute value hangs on an arbitrarily chosen epoch, and the reader has nothing to relate them to. They remain useful internally, as a check of the Moon's position on the dial. An adjustable epoch would make them comparable between observers, but would complicate the rest of the model for two lines.

Epoch: day 0 of the model is 1 January 2023, 00:00 UTC. It is not a discovered moment, it is a chosen one: a cut in the circle so we can turn it into a line. Everything is computed from it in both directions, forward and back. Astronomically it is arbitrary: it falls on no solstice, equinox, new moon or node. Historically, though, it coincides with several simultaneous inflections: the roll-out of artificial intelligence as infrastructure, the end of cheap money and the fall of Silicon Valley Bank, the second year of war in Ukraine with Europe's energy reordering, China's property crisis and its exit from zero-COVID, the expansion of BRICS. It is as arbitrary as any other frozen point, and just as relevant.
